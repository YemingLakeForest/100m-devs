# Handoff — 2026-08-22

One level, added on top of `HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md`'s five. **Six levels**, scope **1 to
1,000,000 developers**, ten blocks of ten buildings of ten floors.

That handoff's §13 made a claim and left it to be spent: *adding a level costs one more division
and a new `TOP_LEVEL`, because the nesting was built for it.* This is the bill. It came to one
division, a new `TOP_LEVEL`, one new file, and **no LOD threshold moved by a single digit** —
which is the part worth the ink, because it is the second time in a row and that is what makes
it a property of the model rather than a coincidence.

Read `HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md` first. Everything in it still holds; nothing here contradicts it.

## 2. The composition

Ten parcels, five across and two back, on the same 2:1 lattice every level below uses. A parcel
holds a whole block at `PARK_SCALE`, so descending into one is a pure scale and there is nothing
to cross-fade — the parcel the address is in *is* `block.ts`, at the size the park draws it.

| | Block units | Park units | Screen px at 997×448 |
|---|---|---|---|
| ten towers (`blockFrame`) | 765 × 629 | 383 × 314 | — |
| **the deck** | **972 × 512** | 486 × 256 | — |
| a parcel's cell (the union) | 972 × 711 | 486 × 356 | **143 × 105** |
| the whole park | — | 2636 × 1431 | **776 × 421** of 997 × 448 |
| one tower, at the park | 115 × 304 | 58 × 152 | 17 × 45 |

### What makes it a park and not more city

§7.8.2 rule 2 is the one rule this level had to be *designed around* rather than tuned into:

> A business park drawn with towers the height of rung 4's reads as **more towers, closer
> together**, which is the one thing a rung change must never look like.

Three things answer it, and none of them is a smaller tower.

**The parcels do not touch — and that is a property of the shapes, not of a margin.** On a 2:1
lattice the cell one column along *and* one row nearer sits at the same x, which is why four of a
block's ten towers stand half in front of four others (`HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md` §13). A block is
**wider than it is tall** where a tower is the reverse, so the very lattice that overlaps towers
separates blocks completely. Neighbours are 6.615 plot strides apart along either lattice axis
and a deck spans 5.24 of them across and 2.24 back, leaving **1.4 strides of boulevard between
columns and 4.4 between rows**. `frames.test.ts` asserts it in lattice coordinates, where the
question has no tolerance in it: two parallelograms of one lattice are disjoint exactly when
their ranges are disjoint on either axis.

The row gap being three times the column gap is the lattice being uniform while the cell is not,
and it is **kept rather than tuned out**. Squeezing the rows to 3.6 strides buys a tighter
bounding box and a ×4.24 step instead of ×4.55, and costs the property above: the near row's
towers start standing in front of the far row's, which is what the *block* looks like and is
precisely what this level exists not to look like. Open ground between the rows is what a
business park has.

**Every parcel stands on a visible plinth.** GDD's own phrase — *"campuses are low-rise on a
visible plinth"* — and it is what turns a cluster of towers into a **site**. A deck is the plot
lattice continued 0.62 of a stride past the plots, drawn as the sheared rectangle the lattice
actually projects to and never as the diamond that contains it (`floorOutline`'s rule two levels
up: the containing diamond is 1,940 block units across against an 860-unit stride, so it would
pave the boulevard, the neighbour, and the neighbour's boulevard). The two edges running down to
the near corner are extruded into faces, with a lit rim along the top of both.

**The ground between them is furniture.** Boulevards along both lattice axes with a centre line,
a car-park apron on the near edge of every deck, and a walkway across the gap between every pair
of neighbours that both exist.

### And a unit is still a unit

§7.8.2 rule 1: *"the promise of §7.7.1 is that the unit changes, not that the same unit gets
smaller — a town occupies exactly as much screen as a building did."* One building at the block
is 77 × 204 px; one block at the park is 143 × 105 px. **15,670 square pixels against 14,983** —
within five per cent, and asserted rather than admired.

## 3. Finding — the frame measured the buildings and not the ground they stand on

Reported by looking at it in a window: the leftmost parcel was cut off by the edge of the screen.

`parkFrame` spans `blockCell()`, and `blockCell()` was `blockFrame(10, 10)` — the ten towers. The
**deck is half again as wide as they are**, 972 block units against 765, because it is a
parallelogram whose corners run past the lattice in *both* directions and each contributes to x
with the opposite sign. So the park framed the towers, `park.ts` drew a site around them, and the
composition was 6% wider than the camera thought.

At 997×448 that is invisible, because the park is height-bound there and 6% of extra width lands
in slack the frame already has. At 1400×900 the fit turns width-bound and the same 6% is a parcel
hanging off the side of the window. **Trap 52 again — the reference frame is a phone in
landscape, and this is the third defect in two handoffs that only exists in a desktop window.**

The fix is `HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md` §13's finding arriving a third time and it is always the same
sentence: **geometry the frame and the drawing both depend on cannot be written down twice.**
`towerSurfaceY` had to move to `frames.ts` because the frame and the drawing were each deriving
the tower's rake; `PODIUM_W` and `TOWER_GROUND` had to move because they were each deriving where
a tower stops; and `DECK_MARGIN`, `DECK_LIP` and `deckOutline` are now there for exactly the same
reason. `park.ts` maps the shape into a parcel and draws it, and owns none of it.

The test that replaced the assumption asserts the containment directly — the cell holds the deck,
the cell holds the towers, and the park holds every parcel — rather than asserting a width.

## 4. Finding — a walkway drawn under the thing it connects is not drawn

The first version of the walkways ran from one deck's centre to the next one's, which is the
shorter and more obvious line, and it was **completely invisible**: the links go down before the
decks (a path under a building is a tunnel), so every part of the line except the gap itself was
painted over — and the part in the gap was a couple of pixels of a much longer stroke that never
appeared.

Drawing them *after* the decks instead would have put a stripe across two of them. What is wanted
is the short piece nobody else is drawing: **edge to edge, across the boulevard, and nothing
else**, anchored at the middle of the lattice edge each deck shares with its neighbour. Six
pixels of `NEUTRAL[3]` at 90% is enough at 143 px a parcel, and it is the mark that separates a
campus from a city.

Two smaller ones from the same pass, both about which way a slab faces:

- **The plinth's visible faces are the two that run to the near corner.** The first pass extruded
  `far → left` — an edge on the *back* of the deck — so the plinth read as being lifted at the
  wrong end. `DECK_FAR/RIGHT/NEAR/LEFT` are named constants now rather than indices 0–3, because
  the whole class of mistake is "which corner was that one again".
- **The selection tick hangs off the near corner**, where nothing else in the picture lives —
  §7's selection lesson, repeated at each level because it is true at each level.

## 5. Finding — `buildingOf` had to go global exactly where `storeyOf` went local

When the block arrived, `storeyOf` changed meaning: it became a storey *of its own building*,
because "floor 14" is not a floor of a ten-storey building. The park asks the same question about
buildings and **the answer is the opposite one**, which is worth writing down because it looks
like an inconsistency and is not.

`buildingOf(seat)` is now park-wide, 0 to 99. The difference is what each number is *for*:

- A storey is only ever a storey of one tower, and every caller — the drawing, the lift line, the
  plate host — wants the local reading.
- A building is the unit `storeysIn` and `devsIn` count people into, and those two want one number
  that walks the whole studio rather than a pair that has to be carried around together and can
  disagree.

So the *block-local* reading gets its own name, `plotOf`, and it is needed in exactly the places
that draw: which of ten plots this building stands on. `buildingAt(plot, block)` and
`seatOfPlot(plot, block)` are the two conversions, and they are functions rather than expressions
at call sites for trap 38's reason — two numberings meeting at a function is fine, two numberings
meeting at a call site is how a picture ends up with one coordinate system and one opinion.

**And `buildingsFor(devs)` was deleted rather than extended.** It answered "how many buildings has
the studio put up", which stopped being a question with one answer the moment there were ten
blocks. `buildingsIn(devs, block)` replaces it and **the block is not optional**: a default of
"the first one" would have made every existing call site quietly mean *block 0*, which is trap 52a
exactly — `seatOfStorey(f)`'s default building is what moved a floor of building 8 to building 1.
The compiler found all six call sites in one pass because there was no default to hide behind.

## 6. Finding — the block's street plane and the park's decks are the same ground

`block.ts`'s ground runs `GROUND_SPREAD` past its outermost plot so that ten towers do not sit on
a raft, and it is sized to fill a whole frame. At parcel scale that plane is **wider than the
boulevard between two parcels** — one block's ground would be painted across its neighbours, and
the picture at the park would be nine decks and one city.

So the block's ground gets a second fade of its own (`setGroundAlpha`), separate from its chrome,
and the two point in opposite directions. The chrome asks *"is the block still the subject"* and
fades as the camera goes indoors; the ground asks *"is this block the whole picture"* and comes
back as the park leaves. At the park you see ten matching decks; at the block you see the street
plane the block level has always drawn. **Two different objects trading places, which §10.5
allows** — the thing it forbids is two pictures of one object, and the towers underneath are
drawn once, by the block, at every level.

The nine parcels the address is not in are faded by the park itself. The one it *is* in keeps its
own chrome fade, because dimming it from the park would dim it twice on the way in.

## 7. Finding — a hundred towers is a hundred towers only once

Ten blocks of ten towers is 100 `drawTower` calls, and one of those is about 160 fills at ten
storeys. Rebuilding the lot on every hire is 16,000 path operations against a §23.3 frame budget
that has to hold 60 fps.

It never happens, and the reason is §7.7.1's own fill order rather than a cache: **blocks fill in
order**, so every block below the last one is full and its answer to `buildingsIn`/`devsIn` never
moves again. `block.setHeadcount` compares *this block's* occupancy rather than the studio's and
early-returns when it has not changed, so a hire at nine hundred thousand developers redraws the
same ten towers it redraws at nine thousand. Measured: **60–61 fps at the park with a million
developers**, at 997×448 and at 1400×900.

## 8. Finding — a probe that aims at the first pick point is aiming at a camera that has moved

Trap 40, met again while writing this handoff's own instrument, and worth recording because the
gate now carries the fix.

The probe swept a grid through `__pick`, kept the first screen point that named each unit, and
tapped it. Every tap at the block level missed. The first point that names a tower is the top of
its crown — the sweep runs top-down — and a sweep of a 997×448 frame is about twelve thousand
`__pick` calls, during which a camera still easing into its stop moves that crown out from under
the cursor. The pick was never wrong. The *aim* was stale by one settle.

It aims at the **centroid** of everything that names a unit now, in the probe and in
`ui-frame.acceptance.mjs`, which is a point in the middle of the thing rather than on its edge and
is robust to the camera moving a few pixels underneath it.

## 9. What it cost to add a level, again

`HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md` §13 measured the fourth level's cost and predicted the fifth's. The
prediction held, and the same four lines are the whole of it:

- **One space, one more division.** `frameFor` returns park space. Every level below the park is
  `intoParcel(…, block)` of what it used to be, and that is the entire change to five levels'
  worth of framing.
- **`floorScaleAt` is the only line that knows.** It gained a `* PARK_SCALE`. Because every LOD
  threshold in the file is expressed in *floor-space* scale rather than camera scale, **not one of
  them moved**: `ROOM_RESOLVE_SCALE`, `BUBBLE_MIN_SCALE`, and the chrome bands are the numbers
  they were, and a desk measures the same 0.2195 of floor scale at the floor level that it did
  before the park existed. That is now asserted in `frames.test.ts` rather than noticed.
- **The ceiling needed nothing.** `maxZoomFor` already returned rung 5 for a studio past a hundred
  thousand and the lens already clamped to `TOP_LEVEL`; moving `TOP_LEVEL` to `PARK` was the whole
  of it. The hundred-thousandth hire lifts the ceiling and `zoomCeilingLifted` flies the camera
  out to the park, scored, with no new code.
- **`unitSeats(5, i)` was already a block.** §7.7.1's rung table has had `{ rung: 5, unitSize:
  1e5 }` in it the whole time, so a tap at the park goes to the store as a unit poke of a hundred
  thousand people with no new arithmetic anywhere.

Measured at the boundaries:

| Developers | Rung | Ceiling | Blocks | Buildings in the last one |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 99,999 | 4 | BLOCK | 1 | 10 |
| 100,000 | 5 | **PARK** | 1 | 10 |

## 10. A naming question, left for a human

At a park, the HUD says two words for one object and the GDD is the reason.

- §7.7.1 rung 4 says what the camera holds at 10⁴–10⁵ is **"a block"**, and `frames.ts` took that
  word: `LEVEL_NAMES[4] = 'BLOCK'`, and the lift panel's rows read `BLOCK 08`.
- §7.7.1 rung 5 says the unit that *arrives* at 10⁵–10⁶ is **"a campus"** — and a campus is 10⁵
  people, which is the same object. `sim/units.ts` took *that* word, so §7.7.5's scale bar reads
  `1 CAMPUS = 100 K DEVS` while the picker beside it is labelling those same hundred-thousands
  `BLOCK 01` … `BLOCK 10`.

They are synonyms rather than a contradiction — nothing on screen is the wrong *size* — and the
fix is one word in one of two tables, so it is cheap either way. It is left alone deliberately,
for two reasons: `sim/units.ts`'s word also names a **hero reach tier** (§13.6.1's ladder is row,
floor, building, campus, town, …) and §13.6.4's VP of Engineering is a Campus card, so changing it
is a content change and not a rename; and `HANDOFF-2026-08-20.md` §18.4 already schedules a dated
rewrite of GDD §7, which is where a canon ambiguity should be settled rather than in a patch.

The level itself is `PARK`, which is rung 5's own *"a business park"* and is the word `sim/ladder.ts`
has used for that rung's view since long before there was one to draw.

## 13. Traps 53–56 — for the register in `HANDOFF.md`

**53. A frame has to span what is *drawn*, not what is *modelled*.** `blockCell` was
`blockFrame`, which is ten towers, and the ground those towers stand on is half again as wide.
This is the third time in two handoffs that a geometry the frame and the drawing both need was
written down twice, and the third time the frame's copy was the wrong one (`towerSurfaceY`,
`PODIUM_W`, now `deckOutline`). **The rule that keeps coming out of it: whichever file draws the
shape does not get to own it — the file the frame is computed in does, and the drawing asks.**

**54. Draw order can make a thing invisible without making it wrong.** The walkways were
correct, tested for existence, and painted over by the decks they connect. Nothing about the
geometry, the colour or the alpha was wrong; the layer was. **A drawing defect that a model
check cannot see is trap 51's sibling** — and the screenshot is still the only instrument that
finds either.

**55. When a function's meaning has to change, ask which direction the *callers* need.** The
block made `storeyOf` local; the park made `buildingOf` global. Those look like opposite answers
to one question and they are the same answer to two different ones: a storey is only ever a
storey *of* one tower, and a building is what people are counted into. Getting it backwards
gives every caller a pair of numbers to carry around together, and a pair that can disagree is
trap 38 with extra steps.

**56. Deleting a function is how you find its call sites.** `buildingsFor(devs)` could have grown
a `block = 0` parameter and every existing call would have compiled and quietly meant *the first
block*, which is trap 52a — a default is the old behaviour wearing the new signature. Removing it
and requiring the argument turned six silent wrong answers into six compiler errors.

## 15. The park rewritten, same day — a board instead of a mat

Everything above §13 describes the park as it shipped this morning: ten parcels in a 5x2 grid on
a 1600-unit ground diamond, boulevards ruled across it, walkways between neighbours. It lasted
one review. The report was one sentence and the diagnosis is inside it:

> *"1m looks bad, they spilled out to what seems to be a mat that does not meet that size."*

**The mat was not cut to the parcels, could not be, and never stopped.** It was fixed-size
scenery, so at any headcount it was either bigger than the composition or smaller, and at ten
blocks it was both at once — running past them on two sides while the near parcels sat on its
edge. Ten blocks read as ten blocks tipped onto a sheet.

### What replaced it

Four compounds of different shapes — four blocks, two, three, one, in fill order — each on its
own **package** with a pin row down its two camera-facing edges, all standing on a **board** that
is cut to its contents and has a near face, a lit rim, silkscreen and mounting holes. A **bus**
runs the two clear channels between the packages with a stub to each, drawn as a symbolic line —
one width, square corners on the lattice, and a node, a hop or an inline bulge wherever something
happens to it. A **substation** and a **cooling plant** fill the open bay, and each arrives with
the compound it serves. Unbuilt blocks are **silkscreened pads**.

Requested as *"several compounds connected with power lines… place them like different parts of
an electronic board"*, and the connectors were requested twice: the first draft drew catenaries
between pylons and was told **no diagonals — symbolic lines with lumps**. That was the right
call and worth writing down. A sagging cable is a *picture* of a power line; at this size it
crosses the composition as a slack curve belonging to no grid in the drawing. The board wanted
the other thing.

### The one number a layout is expensive by

On a 2:1 lattice the projected aspect is **2:1 whatever the arrangement**, so nothing about a
layout is expensive except the sum of the ground's two lattice spans, `U + V`.

| Layout | U + V | Tower | One block |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5x2 grid, as shipped this morning | 40.6 | 16.9 px | 219 px |
| Four compounds, first attempt | 53.4 | 13.1 px | **170 px** |
| Four compounds, packed to budget | 43.9 | 16.0 px | 207 px |

The first attempt cost 22% and **would have failed §7.8.2 rule 1** — a block at the park must
occupy about what a building did at the block, and `frames.test.ts` asserts it between 0.6 and
1.7. What the grouping spends on gaps between compounds it has to buy back by tightening the
decks inside one: `PARK_IN_U` and `PARK_IN_V` are 5.69 and 3.94 against the old 6.615 stride. The
cost as built is 5%.

The back gap stays larger than the across gap and the reason is towers, not ground: a tower is
130 park units tall and one lattice step back drops the next deck 32.5, so rows need more room
than columns or the near one stands in front of the far one — which is what the *block* looks
like and is what this level exists not to look like.

### Four new tests, and what each is for

- **the street inside a compound against the channel between them** — replaces the boulevard test,
  which asserted one gap because there used to be only one kind.
- **no two compounds overlap** — `COMPOUNDS` is the one table a person tunes by hand, so the thing
  a person can break by nudging a number is the thing that gets asserted.
- **everything standing on the board is inside the board** — at every block count, for decks,
  packages and plant, including counts where a plant has arrived and its compound has not filled.
- **the board is laid once and the frame grows** — or the first two blocks are drawn at the scale
  a million people need.

## 16. Traps 57–60 — for the register in `HANDOFF.md`

**57. A ground plane that runs to the frame edge is a backdrop, not a place.** The mat could not
be cut to its contents because it was authored as fixed-size scenery, and a level whose whole job
is to be an object a bigger one will later sit inside cannot be a backdrop. Above the room, every
ground plane is finite, has a near face, and is sized from what stands on it.

**58. A bounding box in projected space projects to a screen-aligned rectangle.** The first draft
of the board built its packages from a park-space bbox, which is arithmetically correct and reads
as a sticker stuck on the picture — the 2:1 world has no axis-aligned rectangles in it. Author in
the lattice, project once. Third restatement of `floorOutline`'s rule, and the first time it was
broken by something that was not a diamond.

**59. Trap 53 has a second half, and the fix walked straight into it.** *A frame has to span what
is drawn* was learned this morning from `blockCell`. Six hours later the replacement cut the board
to the parcels and left the cooling plant hanging off the near edge — the same defect, in the same
level, inside the change that fixed it. **Anything standing on the ground is measured by the
ground**, and the footprint of a thing the frame must span belongs in `frames.ts` with the deck's,
never in the file that draws it.

**60. Where a run sits in the draw stack is not a detail — it is which of two things it is.** A
connector drawn *before* the towers is wiring in a street and is occluded by what stands on it; the
same geometry drawn *after* them is a diagram hung in front of the world. Both are legitimate and
they are different designs. The choice was made explicitly and the file says which one and why.

## 17. And a palette finding — passing `art:check` is not belonging

The board was drawn in `FOLIAGE[0]` for an afternoon. It read unmistakably as a circuit board,
which was the brief, and **the art gate had nothing whatever to say about it**: the colour is in
`master.png`, so every automated question the pipeline knows how to ask came back green.

It was still wrong, and ART_DIRECTION §2.1 says why in its own table — FOLIAGE is *"plants, the
one green thing in the office"*. A board-sized field of it makes the one green thing the largest
object in the game and empties the ramp of meaning for the next plant drawn.

Recorded as ART_DIRECTION §2.1a. **The quantiser polices membership, not fitness.** §5 makes the
*source* of an asset irrelevant to whether it belongs and does that job exactly; it cannot also
decide whether a colour is being used for the thing the column beside it says it is for. A ramp
with a stated job is a decision already made, and spending it elsewhere is an amendment — dated,
deliberate — not a build-time question.

The board is `NEUTRAL[1]` with a `NEUTRAL[0]` shade over it, the packages `NEUTRAL[2]`, the decks
`NEUTRAL[3]`. Three steps of one ramp, and the shade is there because §6's bloom lifts everything
toward white and closes a gap of one step — without it the board, the packages and the decks
arrive on screen as one flat sheet, which is what the mat looked like in different geometry.

## 18. Trap 61 — a `Graphics` cannot be asked whether it crosses anything

The board shipped and the wiring on it was wrong three ways at once, all of them
found by looking at a screenshot and none of them visible to any test in the repo.

> *"what's wrong with all those connections? they don't connect and a bit all over
> the place?"*

**Every tap was 0.07 to 0.14 strides long** — two to four screen pixels. The
packages already sit hard against the bus (U1's edge is v 7.68, the bus is 8.34),
so the connecting run was shorter than the end nodes drawn on it, and `run` puts a
node at *each* end of whatever it is handed. Four taps rendered as eight bright
dots beside a line. They were not stray marks; they were connectors with no wire
between them.

**The tap's sign was backwards.** It started at `box.v0 - PACKAGE_MARGIN * 0.7`,
half a stride *away* from the package it was tapping. So each run began attached to
nothing. "They don't connect" was literally true, at one end.

**The vertical bus ran through a compound.** `BUS_U = 13.0` is in the channel
between U1 (ends at u 12.43) and U2 (starts at 13.60) — but that channel only
exists in the *far* row. U3's package spans **u 0.00 to 18.12**, so below the main
the line was underneath a package and only its escaping fragments were visible.

**The fix is not the finding.** The finding is that the routing lived in `park.ts`,
where the only artefact is a `Graphics` — an object that cannot be asked whether it
crosses anything, starts on an edge, or is long enough to see. So `busRuns(blocks)`
moved into `frames.ts` as *data*, and the test asks four questions: every run is on
a lattice axis, every run is longer than 0.3 strides, no run crosses a package or
plant interior, and every compound and plant is actually reached.

Reverting each fix fails with the right words:

```
a tap run is 0.14 strides long: expected 0.135 to be greater than 0.3
a spur run passes through box 2: expected true to be false
```

Same rule as the deck outline and the plant footprint, now for the third time in
one file: **geometry somebody has to check does not live only in the drawing.**
Traps 53, 59 and 61 are one trap wearing three coats.

The plant chains rather than taps, incidentally, for a reason the test would also
have caught: the substation and the cooling plant stand in the same bay at the same
u, so two independent taps would have run one straight through the other.

## 19. The board becomes a city — 2026-08-23

> *"instead of a rectangle circuit, make it spread out irregular like a city. as
> in I want the feel of an electronic circuit, but it's still a city. you know
> what I mean, like those environmental friendly city illustration."*

A rectangle is the one shape that can be neither of those. So the ground stopped
being authored.

### The island

It is the **union of what stands on it** — a green apron round each district, a
corridor along each street, an apron round each plant, banks along the canal —
rasterised onto a half-stride grid and outlined as a staircase. Three things
follow and the first is the one worth keeping:

- **§7.8.2a stops being a rule a test has to check.** *"The ground is cut to what
  stands on it"* was learned twice the hard way: once as a mat that did not fit,
  once as a cooling plant hanging off a board that did. It is now a property the
  code cannot express the violation of, because the ground **is** the contents.
- **It grows lobe by lobe.** A town at three hundred thousand, a city with
  districts and isthmuses at a million. The aprons differ per compound — four
  numbers — so the coast has a reason to be a different shape in each quarter.
- **Every step is a half stride**, so the coastline is irregular and never
  off-lattice, and stays exactly as crisp as the towers standing on it.

### The green, and the palette rule read properly

A canal in `GLOW` runs the near shore and turns up the one gap the districts
leave: U3 ends at u 18.12 and U4 starts at 19.40, so 1.28 strides of water fit
there and nowhere else on the island. Trees in `FOLIAGE`, placed from a hash of
the lattice cell so the wood you left is the wood you come back to — lining the
streets at 0.55 strides and standing a lip higher on the districts' podiums.

**FOLIAGE came back, and this time it is doing its stated job.** §17 above
recorded turning the green *board* down because ART_DIRECTION §2.1 files that
ramp as *"plants, the one green thing in the office"*. A tree is a plant. The
rule was never about the colour — it was about whether the ramp is being used
for the thing its own row names.

### And the props stopped telling the joke the plan already tells

The plant was a substation drawn as a capacitor bank and a cooling plant drawn
as a heatsink. *"Those capacitors and those heat dispensing slices are still
there? make it more city'y."* Correct: that is the circuit metaphor said twice,
once by the layout — where it belongs — and once by the objects standing on it,
where it turns a city into a diagram.

They are a **solar farm** and a **wind farm** now, which is where a city of a
million people who care gets its power, and which from far enough back still
read as a die and a row of parts. **The metaphor is stronger for being carried
by the plan rather than by the props.**

## 20. Trap 62 — a `Graphics` costs what is in it every frame

The first island emitted one coastline stroke and one face quad per half-stride
cell, plus a second full pass over every cell for the shade. Correct picture,
and it took the frame from **61 FPS to 30**.

The instinct that produced it was that the ground is *static* — rebuilt only when
`built` changes, which is a few times per career — so its cost is a build cost.
It is not. A `Graphics` is re-submitted every frame; what it holds is what the
renderer pays for sixty times a second, whether or not a single instruction in it
has changed since.

Three fixes, no change to the picture: collinear coast runs merged (about two
hundred pieces down to thirty), the shade folded into the fill's own alpha rather
than a second quad per cell, and a tree drawn as two ellipses instead of three.
Back to 60.

**The rule: the question is not how often you rebuild it, it is how much is in
it.** §7.7.1's fill order already pays for the towers on exactly this argument —
only the last block's occupancy ever changes — and the ground had quietly been
exempted from it because nothing about the ground ever changes at all.

## 21. Trap 63 — "intermittent" is a description, not a diagnosis

`test:walk` item 10 failed with *"the point was not spent — the board still
reads 2 POINTS"*, passed on a re-run, and was written off as a flake. It did it
again days later and was written off again. **Both times by me, and both times
without opening the button.**

It is not a flake. `REACH_POINTS` is 3 and `DEPTH_POINTS` is 1, and the hero
tree's buy button read:

```tsx
disabled={selectedState !== 'live'}
```

Reachable, with nothing about affordable. So a hero holding two points was shown
a **live, enabled** button for a node they could not buy; pressing it ran
`buyHeroTreeNode`, which returned false, and the guide sat there. The walk picks
the first live off-branch node it finds, and whether that is a DEPTH (1 point,
buys) or a REACH (3 points, does not) depends on the board — so it failed about
half the time.

**§10.2a already had the rule and it points the other way from where I read
it.** "A control a player has to be told about is not ready to be on screen" —
and one that looks ready and silently does nothing is worse than one that says
why not. The button is disabled when it cannot be afforded and reads `NEEDS 3`.

The walk now asks for a node that is off-branch **and** buyable, which is what it
meant all along; the old selector encoded half the requirement and the half it
left out was the half under test.

**The trap: a test that fails intermittently is reporting a state that occurs
intermittently.** Re-running until it passes chooses the state where the defect
is absent. Twice I took "green on the second run" as evidence there was nothing
there, when the only thing it evidenced was that the second run took the other
branch.
