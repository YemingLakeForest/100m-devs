# Handoff — 2026-08-24

**A design session. Nothing in `src/` changed.** What exists is a working demo of a rung 6 that
is *not* the rung 6 in `src/`, and the reasoning for replacing it.

Read `HANDOFF-2026-08-23.md` first — this is a rewrite of the thing that handoff shipped, and
the argument only makes sense against it.

Demo, live and drag-able: [`demos/jigsaw-planet.html`](demos/jigsaw-planet.html) — open it in a
browser, or **https://claude.ai/code/artifact/b304d35c-5df8-4bdf-91ef-a56af971949e**. The
geometry, the tiling and the buildings are all computed in the page; nothing in it is drawn by
hand, so what you see is what the renderer would have to produce.

---

## 0. The brief

Six things, in the order they were asked for:

1. The globe **spins** — drag it, like a globe in an atlas app.
2. **No sun.** No terminator, no day, no night side.
3. The globe is cut into **100 tetris-shaped jigsaw pieces**, each a city of a million.
4. **No continents.**
5. The cities **blend into the globe**.
6. You expand **piece by piece**.

Then, across four rounds of looking at it: *the city must not be a cluster of dots*, and the
buildings must **stand along the local vertical**, not up the screen.

---

## 1. The cut — a hundred pieces of exactly one area

`worldMap.ts` today spreads 100 sites on a Fibonacci lattice and draws a campus glyph at each.
That gives near-equal spacing and **no pieces at all** — there is nothing to interlock, and no
tiling.

The replacement cuts the sphere into cells and groups them. Bands in `z = sin(lat)`, because
area is exactly linear in `z`, with the **column count stepping down toward the poles**:

| Band | Rows | Cols | Cells | Pieces | Cell area | Aspect |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Polar cap ×2 | 2 | 10 | 40 | 10 | π/100 | 0.6 – 2.4 : 1 |
| Middles ×2 | 3 | 20 | 120 | 30 | π/100 | 0.6 – 2.0 : 1 |
| Equatorial belt | 6 | 40 | 240 | 60 | π/100 | 0.5 – 0.8 : 1 |
| **Planet** | **16** | — | **400** | **100** | **4π** | no splinters |

Verified before anything was drawn: **400 cells, one distinct cell area.** Four cells to a
piece, so a city is **exactly one hundredth of the planet** — not approximately, not on average.

That number is not a nicety. `frames.ts` already sizes the sphere so that a hundred parks
*exactly tile it* (`SITE_SHARE = sqrt(4π / SITES_PER_GLOBE)`), and `HANDOFF-2026-08-23.md` §2
calls that derived rather than tuned. **This is the first version of the geometry that actually
delivers it.** The Fibonacci lattice never did; it only spaced the centres.

Why the grading. A plain lat/lon grid at 40 columns makes the polar cells **sixteen-to-one
splinters** — twenty of the hundred pieces would be spikes radiating from the poles. Halving the
column count quarters the aspect, and halving the band height with it keeps the area identical,
so the three-band scheme costs nothing and buys a planet with no splinters on it.

## 2. The solver — and why the obvious one thrashes

Each band is tiled with real tetrominoes by backtracking. The first version scanned row-major
over the 6×40 equatorial belt and **blew a 400,000-node budget without finding a tiling**.

Two changes made it essentially backtrack-free:

- **Scan column-major.** The belt is wide and short, so a row-major frontier is 40 cells across;
  column-major it is 6.
- **Prune on region size mod 4.** After each placement, flood-fill the free cells; if any
  connected region's size is not a multiple of four, the branch is dead.

Result: **61 nodes** for 240 cells, all seven shapes present (I, O, T, S, Z, J, L), five bands
solved in under 10 ms total. Each band wraps in longitude, so there is no seam at the back.

Bucketing placements by their **minimum cell index** is what makes "try only placements
containing the first free cell" complete even with the wrap — any placement whose minimum lies
before that cell is already blocked, so it cannot be a missed candidate.

**Territory mode** in the demo is the same grid at double resolution (1,600 cells), grouped by
nearest lattice point instead of solved: sixteen-cell pieces, rounder, still interlocking, still
one hundredth each. It is the live alternative, not a throwaway.

## 3. The city — five drafts, and the four that failed

This is the part worth keeping. Each of these was built, rendered, looked at, and rejected, and
the failures are not interchangeable:

1. **Speckles on a sub-grid.** Small blocks kept or dropped by a threshold rising toward the
   middle. Read as *a rash* — a hundred of them tile the planet in a texture nobody wants to
   look at. Perfect lattice alignment made it worse.
2. **District blocks.** Six to nine chunky rectangles on the city's own grid. Read as *a cluster
   of dots*. Fewer, bigger dots are still dots.
3. **A solid rasterised footprint.** Nested rectilinear masses, staircase edges in the same grid
   language as the pieces. Read as *a patch stuck on* — which is trap 63's original complaint,
   arrived at from the opposite direction.
4. **A building cluster.** Five to nine extruded boxes. Read as *scattered specks*: at the size
   a territory is drawn there is no room for several small things to sit together.
5. **One building.** ✔

The conclusion is a size argument, not a taste one. **A territory is about fifty pixels at rung
6.** Anything that subdivides that into parts produces parts too small to read, and the eye
resolves them as noise. One object big enough to see is the whole settlement — and it stays
countable at a glance, which is what the mark is for.

It also makes the blend rule trivially true. `HANDOFF-2026-08-23.md` §3 is *"everything the
studio built is neutral, everything it stands on belongs to the planet"*, and that rule needed
`terrainStep` shared between two files to keep a campus from becoming a sticker. With one
building there is **no ground to hand off at all**: the territory is the planet's own colour,
unmodified, and the studio contributes a solid standing on it.

**And it grows.** Height scales with how long ago the territory was settled — nine cities'
worth from footings to finished. Drag the headcount and the buildings come up out of the ground
behind the frontier, so the planet carries its own history and your first city is visibly your
oldest.

## 4. The camera — orthographic had to go

`worldMap.ts` is orthographic, and `projectSite`'s header makes the case for it: `z` is at once
depth, foreshortening and visibility, which is why nothing in `globe.ts` needs a depth buffer.

**Buildings break that.** Under an orthographic camera a tower at the middle of the disc points
straight down the view axis and projects to its own roof — only cities near the limb would look
like buildings. The demo uses a perspective camera at 1/`KPER` = 2.2 planet radii.

What that changes:

- The silhouette is the circle where `v.z == KPER`, **not** the equator of the view. You see
  **less than a hemisphere**, which is the honest thing and part of why it reads as a ball.
- Everything scales by `1 / (1 − KPER·ρ·v.z)`, so near things are bigger. Buildings had to be
  scaled down about 40% to compensate.
- `ρ` — distance from the planet's centre in radii — becomes a projection parameter. `ρ = 1` is
  the ground, `ρ = 1 + h` is the top of a building `h` tall. **That is the whole of the third
  dimension**, and it is what makes the extrusion radial.

**The one honest cost.** A building at the exact sub-camera point still shows only its roof.
That is geometrically necessary for a radial extrusion under any camera, and no parameter fixes
it. It affects one or two cities out of thirty visible, briefly, and reads as looking straight
down on that city — which is what you are doing. If it ever offends, the fix is to blend the
extrusion direction toward screen-up, and that was explicitly rejected in review.

Faces are culled and shaded by their **true normals in the camera's frame**; the solid is
convex, so its visible faces never overlap and no per-face sorting is needed. Cities are drawn
far-to-near so a close tower can stand in front of a distant one.

## 5. The light — the pop was the quantiser, not the light

The first pass shaded each territory by how square-on it faced a fixed light, quantised to a
palette step. Reported from the chair as *"why do they change lightings every now and then"* —
and it was real. **The quantiser is per territory**, so as the planet drifts a whole piece
crosses a threshold and jumps a full palette step at once. Regions visibly flip colour. No
choice of thresholds fixes it; finer steps only make the pops smaller and more numerous.

**The shading is now fixed to the screen.** Three hard annuli offset toward the upper left,
clipped to the disc, drawn over the finished surface. Nothing about them moves when the planet
does, so nothing can pop; the steps stay hard-edged, so it is still pixel art and not a
gradient; and a territory's colour becomes a fact about the territory rather than about where
the planet happens to be pointing. It is also cheaper — the whole frame went 3.1 → 2.0 ms.

Buildings keep a normal-based light, because a solid that *doesn't* change tone as it turns
does not read as a solid. Small objects, small pops, and the effect is the one you want.

## 6. What this deletes

| Goes | Arrives | Untouched |
|---|---|---|
| `LAND`'s 25 ellipses, `surfaceAt`, `ICE_LAT` | The graded band grid — 5 zones | `frames.ts`, every divisor, `TOP_LEVEL` |
| `sunAt`, `lightOn`, `DAY_MS`, the 96-step clock | A tetromino solver, run once at boot | The address maths — `siteOf`, `seatOfSite` |
| The terminator and the two gates on it | Drag-to-spin; a contiguous settle order | The park below it; the 37-colour palette |
| The 10-rectangle campus glyph | One building per territory, rising as it grows | All three acceptance gates |

Biomes survive, driven by **latitude alone** — polar caps, tundra, two desert belts, temperate
between. A world without continents still needs a reason to be more than one colour, and each
territory shifts at most one ramp step so it reads as a territory without turning the planet
into a quilt.

Colour budget: **six ground entries and one for the studio**, all already in the 37.

## 7. Traps 69–72 — for the register in `HANDOFF.md`

**69. `ctx.beginPath()` discards the whole path, not the last sub-path.** The seam pass walked
every boundary segment and called `beginPath()` to "drop" one that was round the back — which
threw away every segment collected before it. **The entire jigsaw outline silently vanished**,
and the picture still looked plausible because territories differ in colour. Decide visibility
first, then emit. Nothing errored and no test could have caught it.

**70. A scratch variable took the name of the sine of the pitch.** `drawPlanet` opens with
`var cp = cos(pitch), sp = sin(pitch)`. A later `var sp = []` in the same function scope
reassigned it, so every projection multiplied by `Number([]) === 0` and the planet rendered at
an orientation nobody asked for. It *rendered*, which is why it survived a look. In a file where
short trig names are already taken, scratch buffers get long names.

**71. Quantising a light per object makes whole objects pop.** Covered in §5. The general form:
**if a shading step is chosen per shape and the shape moves, the shape flashes.** Either shade
per fragment, or fix the shading to the screen so the shape moves through it.

**72. A wide, short, wrapped grid dead-ends row-major.** Covered in §2. The frontier of a
row-major scan is as wide as the grid; on a 6×40 belt that is 40 open cells to keep consistent.
Scan along the short axis, and prune on connected-region size mod the piece size.

## 8. What needs a human

**The one decision.** *Tetromino* or *Territory* — both are in the demo, both are exactly one
hundredth a piece. Tetromino is the literal reading of the brief and makes the equal-area claim
visible; Territory's rounder interlocking shapes look more like a world, and with the city down
to a single building it carries more of the picture than it did. **Not settled.**

**One reversal to sanction.** `worldMap.ts` opens with a long argument that the planet must not
spin — *a rung whose geometry moves is a rung the camera cannot be sent to*. That still holds
for everything **below** rung 6, so the spin has to be a property of the top rung only, with
entering a city freezing the orientation to that city exactly as it does today. That comment is
load-bearing and should not be overturned quietly.

**Then, to build it:** the graded grid and the solver replace `buildLattice`; `SITES` becomes
piece centroids, so `siteAt` / `orientationFor` / `projectSite` / `siteAtGlobe` keep working
unchanged. `siteAtGlobe`'s nearest-centroid search stays correct but stops being the *right*
answer — a point is now in a territory or it isn't, and the cell grid can answer that exactly.
The perspective camera is the one change with reach beyond `globe.ts`, because `frames.ts`
assumes a fixed scale at rung 6; the demo sidesteps it by never entering a city.
