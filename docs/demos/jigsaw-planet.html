The gesture is the one already on every other rung: drag pans the level. On a sphere, panning is spinning, so nothing new has to be taught. Picking a city still turns the planet to face it — the drag just means you can go looking first.

The shading is fixed to the screen, not to the planet — three hard bands offset toward the upper left. It makes the sphere read as a ball, and because none of it moves when the planet does, nothing changes colour as you spin. Turn Form off to see what it is holding up.

The cut — why every piece is exactly one hundredth

A hundred pieces of one area

Cut a sphere on a plain latitude/longitude grid and the polar cells become sixteen-to-one splinters. Cut it in equal-area bands whose column count steps down toward the poles and they don’t. Three band widths, forty columns at the equator, twenty in the middles, ten at the caps — and the cell area comes out identical everywhere, to the last decimal.

Four cells to a piece, so a city is exactly one hundredth of the planet — not approximately, not on average. That is the number frames.ts already claims when it sizes the sphere so a hundred parks tile it, and this is the first version of the geometry that actually delivers it.

The pieces come out of a tetromino solver run once per band. All seven shapes turn up — I, O, T, S, Z, J, L — and each band wraps around the planet, so there is no seam at the back. Territory mode is the same grid at double resolution, grouped by nearest lattice point instead: sixteen-cell pieces, rounder, still interlocking, still one hundredth each.

The blend — the rule that kills the sticker

The ground is never the studio’s to paint

Before you buy it

After

Same piece, same seam, same ground, same colour. Buying a territory does not repaint anything — it puts one building on it, standing up out of the ground it stands on. Nothing is laid over the top, and there is no footprint whose shape could be wrong, which is the honest end of the argument that started with a grey rectangle stuck on a green world.

And it grows. A territory settled ten cities ago is finished; the one you just bought is still footings. Drag the headcount and the buildings come up out of the ground behind it, so the planet carries its own history — your first city is visibly your oldest.

Six for the ground, in bands. One for the studio. That is the entire colour budget of rung 6, and none of it is new — every entry is already in the 37.

Four richer glyphs went in the bin on the way here — speckles, which read as a rash; district blocks and a building cluster, both of which read as a handful of scattered specks; and a solid footprint, which read as a patch stuck on. At the size a territory is drawn there is no room for several small things to sit together, so one building big enough to see is the whole settlement. It stays countable at a glance, which is what the mark is for.

One decision reverses. worldMap.ts currently opens with a long argument that the planet must not spin — that a rung whose geometry moves is a rung the camera cannot be sent to. That still holds for anything below rung 6, so the spin has to be a property of the top rung only: entering a city freezes the orientation to that city, exactly as it does today. Worth saying out loud before it gets written, because that comment is load-bearing and I would be overturning it.

Your call

Three things I’d want settled

  1. Tetromino or Territory? Tetromino is the literal read — sharp, gridded, unmistakably a puzzle, and a bit mechanical. Territory is softer and more world-like. Both are exactly one hundredth a piece. I lean Tetromino: you asked for Tetris, and it makes the equal-area claim visible.
  2. Does the planet keep colour bands? Right now latitude still drives the palette, so there are green belts, dusty belts and ice caps — a world without continents. The alternative is one flat colour and pure puzzle. Bands, I’d say.
  3. Does growth have to stay contiguous? Cities currently unlock outward from the first one, so your empire is always one connected blob. That reads as colonising. Letting a player jump anywhere reads as a map screen.
