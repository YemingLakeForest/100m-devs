01 The read

Six things the reference does, five of which are free

What makes that video read as a place rather than as a diagram is not fidelity — it is nearly all flat colour, and the people are white capsules. It is six compositional decisions, and we are currently doing one and a half of them.

What we already do: the cutaway shell, and half of the structural rhythm. What we do not: the coloured cap strip, the one dark room, crowd read as texture rather than as a thousand individuals, and — the big one — any ground at all outside the slab.

1 · The shell is cut open, not removed

No roof, but the perimeter is still stated: short wall stubs, a kerb line where the wall would be, and full-height columns standing proud of it. You read a floor plan that has volume. We have two window walls and then nothing — the other two sides just stop, which is why the floor reads as a slab.

2 · Every partition wears a coloured cap

Three pixels of saturated colour on the top edge of each low wall. That single strip is what lets forty rooms be told apart in a 960-pixel-wide frame, and it costs one extra quad per wall. It is the highest-value idea in the whole reference and it drops straight into wallQuad().

3 · One dark room

Among pale rooms, one near-black room with a glow in it reads as the important one at any zoom, with no label. We already own the perfect candidate and have not used it this way: the server room. Same trick, opposite polarity — in our dark world the special room is the bright one, which is what the LEADERSHIP suite's lit glass should be.

4 · Crowd is texture

At the widest zoom the reference stops drawing people and starts drawing density. We draw a thousand real Containers at 59 fps, which is an achievement, and at floor zoom it buys us a grey shimmer. The fix is not fewer people — it is giving the eye something to measure the density against, which is what corridors, islands and columns do.

5 · Structural rhythm

Columns on a fixed pitch, tying the whole floor to one building. Our squad grid already has the pitch — SQUAD_STRIDE_COLS — and nothing standing on it.

6 · The building never touches the frame edge

There is always road, kerb, tree and parking bay on at least two sides. Not one of those objects is expensive. This is where nearly all of the reference's immersion actually lives, and it is the next section.

The one thing not to take

The palette. That reference is near-white with saturated signage, and ART_DIRECTION §1 puts our world behind CRT glass — warm, neutral, dense, lit from top-left, with the one-hue-at-a-time saturation reserved for the interface. Take the composition and the density. Leave the white; a bright office would put the HUD's phosphor and the world on the same footing, which is the exact failure §0 of that document exists to prevent.

02 The thesis

One ground plane, from the driveway up

Right now the room floats in pure black. Everything outside the slab is #14121a and nothing else, at every headcount from one developer to a thousand. That void is doing more damage than any missing prop: a lit rectangle on nothing reads as a UI panel, and the moment you put a kerb and a road under it, it reads as a building.

So the proposal is not "add an exterior to the office." It is stronger and cheaper than that: draw the ground once, and never change what it is. The garage's driveway meets a road. The small office's forecourt meets the same road. The thousand-person floor's car park meets the same road. You never left the street — the building just ate it.

Three moments on one continuous kerb line. Left: the garage, one lamp, one car, night. Centre: the walls have pushed out past the driveway; the car has company; the tree survived. Right: the plot is consumed — bays where the lawn was, the tree line pushed to the boundary, the road unchanged. Same objects throughout. Nothing here is authored twice.

That continuity buys a second thing for free, and it may be the best idea in this document: every amenity gets a garage ancestor. The cafeteria is not a new object at rung 5 — it is the pizza boxes on the car bonnet, promoted five times. Which means the amenity program is not decoration bolted onto the late game; it is the same five jokes told at increasing budget, and the player who sees the barista bar at eight hundred developers has been watching that gag since the kettle on the paint tin.

03 Canon

This contradicts §7.8.1, and we are doing it anyway

GDD §7.8.1 — population steps, the 301–1,000 row

The table's last row reads, in the column headed What arrives with it: Nothing. There is no space left to add anything. And the paragraph under it: The props stop arriving and then start disappearing — the dividers go, the plant is gone, the walkway is desks now. The room gets worse as it gets fuller.

A cafeteria, four kitchenette islands, eight water points and a leadership suite at a thousand developers is the direct opposite of that row. Per CLAUDE.md the latest instruction is canonical, so this is what gets built — and GDD.html §7.8.1 gets amended in the same batch that builds it, not appended to.

But the amendment should keep the thesis, because the thesis is right and it is §6 of the whole design. The reconciliation is one word: congestion, not deletion.

The room getting worse as it fills was only ever implemented as subtraction. Subtraction is the weakest available reading, and it makes the 301–1,000 band — which is the Act IV image, the picture the game has been climbing toward — the emptiest frame in the product. A floor that has had things taken off it is a floor with less to look at.

Whereas: the amenities arrive, and the queues arrive with them. Six people around a kitchenette island meant for two. A water point with a line. A cafeteria bench at four hundred percent occupancy with people eating standing up in the corridor. The corridor crossing that was the floor's one piece of breathing room, jammed solid. The company keeps installing things and the room keeps getting worse, and those are the same sentence. That is a far crueller picture than an empty slab, and it is a much better one.

It also survives contact with the existing code: propsAt() already has a crowded flag at devs >= 80 that currently only adds cardboard boxes. Everything above is that flag, applied to occupancy counts instead of to prop existence.

04 The office

A thousand a floor, and not one seat moved

The floor is already ROOM_SQUAD_COLS = 5 by ROOM_SQUAD_ROWS = 2 squads of a hundred. Between them run CORRIDOR_COLS = 2.6 and CORRIDOR_ROWS = 2.0 of empty floor, and around them FLOOR_AISLE_COLS = 4 of empty margin. Almost the whole amenity program fits in space that already exists and is currently blank.

Two things do ask for room. The plaza needs the transverse corridor opened from 2.0 to about 5.0 — uniformly, so no plate moves — which costs 3.3 grid units of floor depth. And brick-bonding the back row costs 3.0 units of width. Both are shell growth. Neither is a seat.

That matters for more than tidiness. §7.8.1b fixes that developers arrive row by row and seat n is always the same seat; moving the grid to make room for a canteen would move everybody already sitting in it. Putting the program in the hallways and the perimeter costs zero seats, so seatFor(), the hero plots and the test:room containment proofs all stand.

Correction — the first sketches had the grain wrong

They laid desk rows along the wrong floor axis at a near-uniform pitch, which is exactly a phalanx: a square block of identical figures with no aisle and no front. The shipped renderer does not do that.

isoAt(col, row) returns gridToScreen(row * PITCH_ROW, col * PITCH_COL). A row runs south-west along gy at PITCH_COL = 1 — desks butted shoulder to shoulder, no gap — and the next row steps south-east along gx at PITCH_ROW = 2.1, which is the aisle. One tile along a row, 2.1 behind it. That ratio is the floor's grain, it is already in the code, and it is most of the answer on its own.

It also changes what a plate is. A hundred seats at ten by ten is 10 tiles across and 21 deep — a ribbon running away from the camera, never a square. Every sketch below is redrawn on it.

Rows run along gy at one tile a seat; rows step along gx at 2.1 — isoAt()'s own arrangement, so the aisle behind every row is real. No plate is a rectangle: five rows of twelve on QA, seven ragged ones on SRE, a bite out of the eighth for a huddle, and the tenth still filling. The spine runs the width of the floor with the plaza on it; four ribs cross it, each with a kitchenette at the T.

05 Variation

Seven ways to stop a floor reading as a car park

The lattice is the real problem with the floor, and it is not fixed by hanging amenities around the edge of it. Ten identical plates on a regular grid, each of ten identical rows, is a car park of car parks.

§7.8.1 already diagnosed this one scale down, and its words fit exactly: a single uniform pitch reads as a lattice of identical objects with no front, no grain, and no indication of which way anyone is meant to move through it. The fix there was to split the pitch — tight along a row, generous behind it. The fix here is the same fix applied one level up: split the pitch between plates, and give the plates an identity.

  • PitchBrick bond. The back row of plates steps half a stride — 3.0 units — relative to the front. The longitudinal corridors stop being straight canyons running the whole depth of the floor, and the crossroads become T-junctions. Costs 3.0 units of shell width. Costs no seats.
  • OutlineNo plate is a rectangle. Rows run 7 to 12 seats instead of 10 every time, the ends are ragged, and where a hallway meets a plate a bite comes out of it for a two-chair huddle. This is the direct answer to the legion: the grain inside a plate was already right, and it was the plate's outline that made it a formation. The ragged pattern is fixed per row index, not solved per headcount — §7.8.1b's whole point is that a row which widens moves everybody already sitting in it.
  • ColourRole trim on the plate edge. §4.9 seats roles together and §4.15 already owns a colour vocabulary; branchColour() has six of them. A QA plate is amber-edged, an SRE plate red, Support green, engineering the cream. This is the reference's cap strip carrying real information: at floor zoom the trim is an org chart.
  • StateThe filling plate looks unfinished. The newest squad draws bare desks with no chairs and a stack of monitor boxes in the corner. §7.8.1b promises that hire n takes the next seat in the row, and nothing on screen currently shows you which seat that is.
  • PropsFurniture inside the floor, not only against the walls. wallSpot() puts every prop on a perimeter, which is exactly why the middle is a lattice. A whiteboard at the end of a row, a plant on a plate corner, a coat rack, a recycling station, a printer island in the aisle.
  • EdgeThe window row is different work. Plates against the glass get standing desks and a ledge facing out. One rule, and it stops the perimeter and the core being the same picture.
  • MiddleThe plaza. The next two sections.

Three widths, and only two of them are hallways

The other half of the fix, and the one that actually shapes the plates. At the moment a "corridor" is an absence of desks — the same floor colour with nothing standing on it. That is not a hallway, it is a gap, and a floor made of gaps between blocks is a parade ground.

A hallway becomes an object when it has five things, none of them expensive: its own floor finish with a defined edge where it meets a plate; a run of beams overhead with light strips in them, which reads even in a cutaway; a destination at each end, so it is going somewhere rather than merely being empty; something along one side — lockers, a noticeboard, vending, bikes, a printer bay; and a sign at each junction, hung, in the colour of the zone it points at.

Then there is a hierarchy, which is what stops every gap looking equally important:

  • Spine · 4.2One, running the full width of the floor. The lightest finish, the beam run, the wayfinding. Reception at one end, the lift core at the other, the plaza in the middle of it.
  • Rib · 1.8Four, running front to back off the spine. Plate edge to plate edge, terminating in glass. Where a rib meets the spine you get a T-junction — which is a better place for a kitchenette than a crossroads, because a T has a wall behind it.
  • Slip · 1.05The aisle behind every desk row. PITCH_ROW minus the desk. This is not a hallway and should not be dressed as one — naming it separately is how the other two stay meaningful.

Floor finish, edge, beam run, wall furniture, hung signage — and a plate on each side with its rows running away along gy, which is what a bank of desks actually looks like beside a corridor. The slip is in the key to be excluded from it.

Both hold the same hundred seats in the same arrival order, on the same grain. Left: seven rows of ten, no edge, no props — a rectangle, repeated ten times, which is the formation. Right: the same seats with ragged ends, a bite taken out for a two-chair huddle, an amber QA edge, a squad sign on the corner column, and four pieces of furniture standing in the floor rather than against a wall.

06 The plaza

A ring of sofas, and one thing worth looking at

The transverse corridor opens from 2.0 grid units to 5.0 along its whole length — uniformly, so not one plate moves — and the plaza takes the middle of it. Circulation on a floor of a thousand people wants that width anyway; the plaza is what the width is for.

Why an octagon

Because it is the only rounded shape this projection gives you for free. An octagon laid out in grid space puts four of its edges on the floor axes — screen slope ±0.50, the only legal value — and four on the floor's own diagonals, which come out exactly vertical and exactly horizontal on screen. Every edge is a real direction in the floor plane. A circle approximated in screen pixels would be the same class of mistake as the kite-shaped floor plate: a shape that is not lying on the ground.

What is in the ring

Four sofas, one on each axis edge, backs outward and facing in. Four pouffes on the diagonals, because a sofa cannot sit on a diagonal edge without leaving the projection. Four planters outside the ring on the same diagonals, two low tables, a laptop somebody left open, and a mug nobody has taken back.

And it has a job

This is the part that matters mechanically. §7.8.6's errands and the slack-off walkers need somewhere to go, and right now the only destinations on the floor are a water cooler and a wall. The plaza is a real one, and every person sitting in it is a person not at a desk — the entropy argument standing up and walking across the room. The cafeteria queue makes the same argument at the edge of the floor. The plaza makes it in the middle, where you cannot miss it.

Occupancy is the dial, exactly as it is for the benches: at a hundred developers the ring is half empty and reads as a perk. At a thousand every seat is taken, people are perched on the planter edges, and one side of the ring is closed — which is the next section.

Octagon of radius 2.5 in a corridor 5.0 deep, with the plates either side exactly where they were. The gilded rung brings four floor uplighters and a velvet rope, and the rope closes the south sofa.

07 The plinth

The founder, cast — and the rope that closes a sofa

The vanity item in the middle is a statue of you, and the reason it should be that rather than a fountain or an abstract sculpture is that it is the only object in the game that can be personal without being authored. It is built from the same two boxes a developer is, at 2.4× scale, out of the look the player already chose in game/founderProfile.ts — body shape, hair, shirt. No two runs get the same statue and nothing has to be drawn.

It holds the studio's first shipped release aloft, gold-plated. The plaque reads that game's real title and its real §4.14 rating, pulled from the save: FLAPPY SQUARE 1.0 — 6/10. And it faces the leadership suite in the north corner. It has its back to the floor.

Five rungs, one slot

Same device as the LEADERSHIP sign, deliberately — one gag engine, told in materials, is a house style rather than a repeat.

Why the rope is the whole point — §7.8.5

The GDD names one detail it explicitly asks to protect: crowding removes the things somebody chose — the plants, the sofa — and adds more of the thing nobody chose, the unpacked cardboard. A room that merely emptied would read as a budget cut.

The gilded rung is that rule, made specific and made personal. The sofa is not taken away by crowding. It is taken away by you — roped off so that a thousand people have somewhere to not sit while they look at a gold statue of the person who hired them. That is a crueller version of the detail the document is protecting, and it survives the amendment §03 already needs.

Does it do anything?

No, and it should say so. The plaza has a function — it is where the errand walkers go. The statue has none: it costs real cash, produces nothing measurable, and the purchase card should state that in one line rather than inventing a morale stat to justify it. Keeping the two apart is what makes the joke land, because the useful thing is the sofas and the expensive thing is sitting in the middle of them taking up the room. If you would rather it carried a small entropy effect, say so — but my recommendation is that it does not.

The plinth is a slot

One geometry, five occupants; the statue is only the default. Whatever stands there is the only object in the middle of the floor, so the choice is visible at floor zoom without a label — which is what makes it worth choosing.

Top row: the statue's five budgets on the same plinth. Bottom row: the alternates. The original desk — the garage desk itself, under glass, monitor still on, which is §02's continuous ground finished as an object. The monolith — a black slab etched with the first commit hash; people touch it going past. The rocket — the release icon from the HUD, three metres, on gimbals. The trophy case — a shelf per shipped title, empty shelves included. The fountain — the only water on the floor that is not a cooler, which finishes the water-point joke.

08 Leadership

Extruded letters, on a wall the projection allows

The sign is asked for as floating 3D text on the wall, and there is exactly one constraint that decides how it is built: a plane lying in this projection runs at a screen slope of ±0.50 and there is no third legal value. That is the defect the room shipped once already — three glass panes at slopes of −0.39, +1.18 and −1.05 — and it is what test:room now exists to catch.

So the letters are drawn as a sheared text run on the wall's own plane, extruded by repeating it along the wall normal in four to six steps, darkest at the back. That gives real depth, stays exactly on the legal slope, and costs one Text object plus a handful of tinted copies. The sign stands SUITE_SIGN_OUT = 0.3 tiles proud of the wall, which is already a constant in room.ts — it just has no depth yet.

Top: the suite at floor scale — glass on two sides, opaque on the two that are the room's own back walls, boardroom table, twelve chairs, a wall screen, and the sign standing off the opaque wall on the legal slope. Bottom: the same word at five budgets. One gag, six hundred developers long.

The material ladder is the joke and it should be built as one object with five skins, not five objects. Cardboard, sagging, hand-lettered, in the taped square on the garage floor. Then whiteboard marker. Then printed vinyl on the first real partition. Then backlit acrylic. Then, at the top, extruded brushed steel with a rim light, on the glass suite of a floor holding a thousand people — the same word, in the same place, that somebody wrote on a box.

Legibility floor

Per §7.8.13's plate rule, the sign is set in the one pixel face at its own design size and never below it. A resampled pixel glyph is not fainter, it is different — the plates came out of the renderer reading I-EANFRAHTP the first time. So LEADERSHIP does not shrink with zoom; it drops to LEAD, then to the lit box alone. At floor zoom the question has already stopped being "what does that say" and become "what is that lit box in the corner", and the box is the answer.

09 Amenity

The cafeteria is a queue with furniture around it

A canteen drawn as empty tables is set dressing. A canteen drawn as a servery with a line coming out of it is a mechanic you can see — and it is where §7.8.6's errands and the slack-off system already want somewhere to go. Every person in that queue is a person not at a desk, which is the entropy argument standing up and walking across the floor.

The kitchenette island

Four of these, one at each T-junction where a rib meets the spine, and they are the most-used object on the floor after a desk. An island is the right shape for the position: people stand on all four sides of it and traffic still passes, which is not true of anything pushed against a wall.

The island: sink and tap, kettle, a mug tree of mismatched mugs, a bowl of fruit nobody eats, a stack of takeaway cups, and a waterfall end. The run behind: tall fridge, worktop, microwave, bean-to-cup machine, wall units. Three bins in a row, colour-capped, and the recycling one is overflowing. Three stools on the hallway side, two of them occupied by people who are not working. Two pendants over the island are the only light source in the vignette — which is what makes it read as a room within a room rather than a counter in a void.

Left to right along the near edge: servery (hot counter, sneeze glass, tray rail, two staff behind), bench seating at 2 × 6 with the far bench already full, kitchenette island with kettle, sink and a bin that is overflowing, water point with two bottles and a queue of three. Occupancy is the dial: at 100 developers the benches are half empty and it looks like a perk; at 1,000 people are eating standing up in the corridor.

10 Outside

Kerb, lane dash, four trees, one lamp

The exterior is a shallow apron ringing the slab, and it is deliberately shallow — this is Level 1, and §7.7.1 gives streets and towers to the block level. What goes here is the immediate ground: enough to say the building stands somewhere, and not one object more.

On the two open sides: forecourt, kerb, footway, road with centre dashes, parking bays, tree line, lamps. Behind the two window walls: a three-layer parallax band — near rooftops, a lit road, distant towers — because the floor is up a tower and the honest view out of it is a view down. Both are strips, not scenes. The tree is four boxes.

Scale check

At a thousand developers the slab is roughly 66 × 26 grid units. An apron that reads at all needs about 6 units of depth on the two open sides, which is a 10% growth in the camera's content box — inside what ROOM_CONTENT_PAD_X = 84 / PAD_Y = 48 already reserve for props standing past the shell. The apron should be part of that pad rather than a new fit rule, or the camera will start cropping the founder again.

11 The garage

Simpler, and funnier, and it needs the leadership room most

The garage is where the whole ladder is established, so it earns more than it currently has. It already owns the bones — door, slab, block walls, workbench, shelving, beer fridge. What it is missing is outside, and the joke.

Outside: cracked drive apron, kerb, road, mailbox, wheelie bin, one tree, one sodium lamp, the neighbour's fence, the hatchback. Night — the lamp and the monitor are the only two light sources in the entire game at this point, which is §7.8.1's first row taken literally. Inside, along the bottom: the leadership room's first three states.

The leadership room in a garage

It cannot be a room, which is exactly why it should exist. Three states before the first real wall:

  • 3–5 devsA taped rectangle on the concrete. One folding chair inside it. A cardboard sign against the wall, hand-lettered, sagging, with the P running out of room. Nobody sits in it.
  • 6–10 devsA shower curtain on a rail round the corner by the fridge. The sign is now whiteboard marker, and it has been rewritten straighter. Somebody is in there.
  • 11–30 devsThe first real partition — one glass panel and a door, standing on the concrete with the tape still visible under it. Printed vinyl sign. This is the panel the office suite is built from, and it never leaves.

The tape staying visible under the glass for the rest of the game is the single cheapest piece of storytelling available to us, and it is one polygon.

What else the garage should get

All of these are the garage-grade ancestors from §02, and each one is two or three boxes: the kettle on an upturned paint tin beside the fridge; the garden hose coiled by the side door, which is the water place; a tower of pizza boxes on the car bonnet that grows a box per shipped release; a whiteboard leaning against the wall rather than hung, because nobody has drilled it yet; and a basketball hoop over the up-and-over door, outside, with no net.

12 Order

What to build first, and which gate will argue

Ranked by legibility gained per line changed. The first two are most of the effect.

  1. The ground plane and the kerbKill the void. A ground quad, a kerb line, a road band with dashes, four trees and two lamps, sized off the shell and growing with it. Largest single change to how the room reads, and it touches no game state. Argues with: the camera fit — fold the apron into ROOM_CONTENT_PAD, don't add a rule.
  2. The coloured cap strip, carrying roleOne extra quad on the top edge of every wall, partition and plate kerb, in the role colour where the zone has one. Turns ten grey plates into a readable org chart and the suite, server rooms and pods into four distinguishable objects. Argues with: nothing. Additive, one function.
  3. The hallways — spine, ribs, and a floor finishThe highest-value change on this list after the ground plane, because it is what makes every other zone read as being somewhere. A finish, an edge, a beam run, wall furniture, hung signage. Argues with: walkPath.ts, which should route down these rather than through the desks.
  4. Ragged plates and brick bondThe two changes that kill the formation, and neither moves a seat or reorders an arrival: a fixed per-row length table instead of a constant ten, and the back row stepped half a stride. Argues with: test:room plate containment, and 3.0 units of extra shell width.
  5. The plazaWiden the transverse corridor to 5.0 uniformly, cut the octagon, ring it with four sofas and four pouffes, and point the errand walkers at it. Useful before the plinth exists. Argues with: CORRIDOR_ROWS, the camera re-fit, and walkPath.ts, which now has a destination worth routing to.
  6. The plinth, at rung 0Ship the empty plinth and the taped A4 first. It is three boxes, it is funny on its own, and it makes the vanity slot visible before there is anything to put in it.
  7. Structural columns on the squad pitchColumns floor-to-cut-line on SQUAD_STRIDE, plus the squad sign at the top of each. Argues with: containment — columns stand at plate corners, which is exactly where the plate edge is.
  8. The statue, rungs 1–4One builder, five branches, reading founderProfile and the first release from the save. The rope closing the south sofa is part of rung 4, not a separate feature.
  9. The extruded LEADERSHIP signShear on the legal ±0.50 slope, five to six tinted copies along the wall normal. Argues with: test:room's slope assertion, which is the point.
  10. Corridor program: islands and water pointsFour islands at the T-junctions, eight coolers at the mid-spans, driven by a zone table rather than propsAt()'s flat list. Argues with: walkPath.ts — the lanes run down those corridors and now have obstacles in them.
